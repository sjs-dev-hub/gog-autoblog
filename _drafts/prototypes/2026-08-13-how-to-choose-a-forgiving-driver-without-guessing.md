## Begin with the miss you need to manage

A driver can only be helpful if its design addresses what happens during your swing. Mark the face during a practice session and note whether contact clusters toward the heel, toe, crown, or sole. Also record whether the starting direction and curve are consistent. That evidence is more useful than shopping from a broad label such as game improvement, because two golfers can create the same slice through different combinations of face angle and swing path.

**Guild recommendation:** Bring a simple impact map and a description of your normal ball flight to a fitting or demo session. Compare dispersion before chasing the single longest shot.

## Treat loft and shaft as part of the head

Loft, delivered loft, strike location, and shaft behavior work together. A lower printed loft does not guarantee more distance, and a stiffer shaft is not inherently more accurate. Compare options using repeatable swings and look at launch, spin, carry, landing angle, and left-to-right dispersion. Adjustable sleeves are useful for testing, but changing a setting can affect more than one playing characteristic, so document each setup rather than turning the sleeve at random.

**Guild recommendation:** Choose the combination that produces a useful launch window and predictable misses across several swings, not the configuration attached to the fastest swing.

## Use forgiveness claims as a shortlist, not a verdict

Manufacturers describe stability with terms such as high MOI, perimeter weighting, or draw bias. Those features can help retain ball speed or influence face behavior, but they cannot replace suitable length, total weight, swing weight, loft, and shaft. Confirm that any club you plan to use in competition conforms to the applicable equipment rules, then test whether the complete club improves your pattern compared with your current driver.

**Guild recommendation:** Shortlist two or three conforming heads, test them with comparable shafts, and keep the current driver in the session as a baseline.

## Build a five-swing strike map

Use your current driver, one ball model, a consistent tee height, and face spray or impact tape at a safe practice range.

<ol>

<li>Choose one target corridor and make five normal swings rather than trying to manufacture extra speed.</li>
<li>Mark the strike location and note the starting direction and curve after every shot.</li>
<li>Circle the pattern you produced most often and use that ordinary pattern as your fitting baseline.</li>

</ol>

## The practical takeaway

- Map strike location and ball flight before selecting a driver category.
- Compare dispersion, carry, and launch across repeated swings rather than selecting the longest single result.
- Evaluate the complete club; head marketing cannot account for shaft, length, weight, or individual delivery.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does high MOI automatically make a driver straighter?

It can improve stability when impact misses the center, but starting direction and curve still depend heavily on face delivery, path, strike location, loft, and fit. Treat MOI as one useful design attribute rather than a guarantee.

### Should a slower swinger always choose more loft?

Not always. More loft can help some golfers create a better launch window, but strike location and delivered loft matter. Compare measured results with the same ball under similar conditions.
