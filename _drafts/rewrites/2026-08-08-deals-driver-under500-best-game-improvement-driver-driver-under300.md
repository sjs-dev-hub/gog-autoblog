When tee shots alternate between a low runner, a towering miss, and a playable drive, it is tempting to buy a new head, a launch monitor, and a swing aid at once. Start smaller: make driver loft the one variable you compare, record what the ball does, then decide whether a fitting or another purchase can answer the remaining question.

## The quick verdict

Do not solve an unclear driver flight by stacking purchases. Establish a repeatable strike and flight baseline, compare one loft setting or one lofted head at a time, and use a fitting when your observations still do not identify a clear choice.

## The upgrade mistake: buying answers before defining the question

A golfer sees one drive fly too low, another balloon, and a third leak away from the fairway. The usual response is to search for a lower-loft head, a higher-loft head, a new shaft, a draw-oriented model, and a monitor. Those purchases can overlap because none has yet been assigned a specific job.

Make loft the first equipment question, not the first purchase. Its job is narrow: help you compare the launch picture you see when tee height, ball, target, and swing intention stay as stable as possible. Strike location and ball flight are not distractions from that decision; they are the record that tells you whether the comparison means anything.

**Guild recommendation:** Do not add a second driver, a swing aid, or a monitor until you can state what your present driver does on a typical solid strike and what you want to compare.

## Read the flight in the right order

Begin with strike. A shot struck in a different part of the face than the previous shot is not a clean loft comparison, even if both shots finish near the same place. Then describe launch simply: low, medium, or high relative to your own normal flight. Finally, note the ball’s starting direction and general curve.

This sequence keeps a golfer from calling every disappointing drive a loft problem. For example, if a player’s best-centered strikes repeatedly launch lower than the flight they want to investigate, that is a useful reason to compare an adjacent loft setting. If the marks scatter around the face and the flights range from low to high, the useful outcome is not a loft verdict. It is a clearer baseline for a fitter.

**Guild recommendation:** Use plain, repeatable descriptions. A notebook entry such as “high-face strike, medium launch, started right” is more actionable than “felt great but went nowhere.”

## Change one variable, then return to neutral

The strongest home comparison is deliberately boring. Keep the same ball, tee height, target, and intent. Compare only two adjacent loft settings, or compare your current driver against one alternate-loft head. A larger menu of changes creates a story, not evidence.

The return set matters. Suppose the higher setting looks promising for five swings. Put the club back where it started and hit another group. If the original pattern disappears, the earlier result may have been driven by changing contact rather than the setting. If the pattern reappears, you have something concrete to discuss in a fitting.

There is no universal number that belongs in every golfer’s bag. PGA of America guidance describes club fitting as an individual evaluation, which is the appropriate next step when your organized observations still leave two plausible choices.

**Guild recommendation:** Treat a loft setting as a comparison tool. Keep the option that gives you the more repeatable observable result, and seek fitting when the result remains ambiguous.

## Buy in sequence, not in a pile

First, use your current driver and a simple strike-location record. This costs the least in complexity and reveals whether a loft trial is even interpretable. Second, if your driver offers adjustable loft, conduct the two-setting comparison. If it does not, bring the baseline notes to a fitting rather than guessing at a replacement loft.

Third, add a launch monitor only if it will answer a question your written observations cannot answer and you will use it under repeatable conditions. A monitor is not a substitute for controlled setup; it is an additional layer of information. Likewise, a second driver is justified when a fitting identifies it as the individual golfer’s better option, not because several products promise to address the same frustrating tee shot.

Before entering competition, confirm that the club you plan to use conforms to the applicable rules. The USGA states that equipment used in competition must conform to the applicable club rules.

**Guild recommendation:** Upgrade from observation to comparison to fitting, then buy only the item that resolves the remaining decision.

## When loft is not today’s equipment decision

Set the loft project aside when your notes do not repeat. A player who produces scattered face contact and changing start lines may still improve, but a different loft selection cannot be responsibly inferred from a handful of unrelated shots. Keep the current club, repeat the baseline on another day, or take the record to a qualified fitter.

This is also the reason to resist the urge to buy a driver and a training product as one solution. The driver comparison answers an equipment question. Practice answers a repeatability question. Keeping those jobs separate protects both your budget and your decision.

**Guild recommendation:** If the pattern changes every swing, delay the purchase. Your next useful action is better observation, not a more complicated equipment setup.

## One-Variable Tee-Shot Baseline

Use your current driver, the same type of ball for every shot, and a safe practice range. Choose a target line and make three simple notes after each shot: strike location on the face, initial flight height, and the shot’s general curve or starting direction. If your driver has an adjustable loft setting, use only two adjacent settings; otherwise, keep the club unchanged and build the baseline for a later fitting.

<ol>

<li>Hit five tee shots with your normal setup and normal intent. Record only shots that you can clearly observe from strike through the early part of flight.</li>
<li>If your driver has adjacent loft settings, change only the loft setting and hit five more shots with the same tee height, target, and ball. Do not change your swing thought to chase a preferred result.</li>
<li>Set the club back to the first setting and hit a final five shots. This return set checks whether the earlier pattern was repeatable rather than a short-lived run of good or poor contact.</li>
<li>Review the notes by group. Give more weight to the setting that produces a more repeatable strike location and a ball flight you can identify consistently, rather than to one unusually long-looking shot.</li>
<li>If neither setting produces a recognizable pattern, keep your current club and take the notes to a club-fitting appointment rather than buying another driver-related product.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> You finish with a written baseline that either identifies one setting with a repeatable strike-and-flight pattern or clearly shows that a fitting is the next upgrade, not another overlapping purchase.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- Driver loft is best treated as one controlled variable, not a universal prescription.
- Record strike location before interpreting launch and ball flight.
- Use a two-setting comparison and a return set to avoid confusing a short streak with a repeatable pattern.
- A fitting is the sensible next upgrade when organized observations leave more than one plausible loft choice.
- Buy measurement or training tools only when each one answers a distinct question that your current process cannot answer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I choose driver loft from my handicap?

No universal loft choice follows from handicap in this process. Use repeatable observations of strike and ball flight to narrow the question, then use an individual fitting if the choice remains unclear.

### Can I compare lofts without a launch monitor?

Yes. You can record strike location, relative launch height, starting direction, and curve using the same ball, tee height, target, and intent. A launch monitor is an optional later tool, not the starting requirement.

### What should I do if one loft gives one exceptional drive?

Do not make the decision from that shot. Complete the comparison group, return to the original setting, and look for a pattern that survives the return set.

### When should I buy a new driver instead of using an adjustable setting?

Consider a new driver after your baseline and an individual fitting identify a clear reason to replace the current club. If you cannot yet describe a repeatable strike-and-flight pattern, another driver is unlikely to be the first useful purchase.

### Do I need to check whether a driver is legal for competition?

Yes. Equipment used in competition must conform to the applicable club rules, according to the USGA.
