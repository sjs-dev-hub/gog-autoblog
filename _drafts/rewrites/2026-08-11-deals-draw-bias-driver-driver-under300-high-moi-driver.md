When a driver flies low one day and balloons the next, changing loft can feel like a guess. Use a small, repeatable check of launch, face strike, and usable ball flight before asking a fitter to confirm the best setting.

## The quick verdict

Do not choose driver loft from a chart or another golfer’s setting. In a short session, compare one loft change at a time and keep the option that produces the most repeatable launch, face contact, and playable flight for your usual tee shot.

## Start with the tee shot that creates the problem

The decision is rarely “Should I play 9.5 or 10.5 degrees?” It is usually more concrete: a tee shot that never seems to stay in the air on a forced carry, or a high shot that becomes difficult to place when the fairway narrows. Define the shot you want to repeat on an ordinary hole, using your normal ball and normal tee height. Then judge candidate lofts against that task, not against a universal number.

A useful baseline has three visible parts. First, note launch as low, medium, or high relative to the course shot you need. Next, look at strike location over several swings. Finally, record where the ball finishes relative to your target line. This is deliberately simple: it gives a busy golfer a pattern to compare without turning a range visit into a technical project.

**Guild recommendation:** Write down the desired shot before changing a setting, such as “a medium-height tee shot that finishes near the center of the fairway.”

## Treat loft as one change in a controlled comparison

Loft is worth comparing, but it should not be asked to answer every problem at once. A different tee height, a new ball, a changed target, and a new loft setting can produce a memorable result without explaining which change mattered. Keep the station small and the test narrow: one club, one target, one tee height, and two candidate settings.

For example, a golfer with only one open bay and 15 minutes can hit five shots in a familiar setting, switch loft, and hit five more. That is not a final fitting. It is enough to decide whether one option repeatedly makes the intended flight easier to produce. If the groups overlap, that is useful information too: do not force a conclusion from a handful of shots.

**Guild recommendation:** Change only the loft setting during a comparison, and evaluate the two groups rather than chasing the best individual drive.

## Read launch, strike, and flight together

A high or low ball flight by itself is an incomplete answer. Look beside it at the marks on the face and the finishing pattern. If one setting seems promising only when contact is centered, while another produces a more usable flight across your ordinary strike pattern, the second setting may be the more practical starting point. The goal is not to make every drive look identical; it is to make your normal tee shot more predictable.

Avoid using a single flight as a verdict. A drive can look ideal because it caught the center of the face, or look poor because it did not. Five modestly similar flights tell a more useful story than one spectacular shot. If strike marks are scattered broadly, prioritize getting a professional look at the whole club-and-player match before assuming loft is the main lever.

**Guild recommendation:** Keep a setting only when its flight improvement appears alongside a repeatable strike pattern, not just one exceptional strike.

## Use fitting to turn a useful clue into a club decision

The PGA of America advises evaluating equipment choices for the individual golfer through fitting. Your compact comparison creates helpful information for that conversation: which settings you tried, the tee height you used, where contact tended to appear, and what ball flight you were trying to manage. Bring those notes rather than arriving with a fixed loft number in mind.

A fitter can evaluate the complete setup for you instead of isolating the number stamped on the head. For golfers who play in competitions, equipment used must conform to the applicable club rules under the USGA Equipment Rules. Confirm conformity as part of the final equipment decision, especially after any adjustment or replacement.

**Guild recommendation:** Use your practice notes to guide an individual fitting, and confirm that the club you intend to compete with conforms to applicable rules.

## The 15-Minute One-Change Loft Check

Use the same driver, golf balls, target, tee height, and normal pre-shot routine throughout. If the driver offers more than one loft setting, select only two nearby settings to compare. Put one alignment stick on the ground aimed at the target, and use face-impact tape or a removable strike marker if available. Keep a phone or small notebook nearby.

<ol>

<li>Warm up with several unrecorded swings, then choose the driver loft setting you normally use.</li>
<li>Hit five tee shots at the same target. After each shot, note only three items: whether the ball launched low, medium, or high; whether it finished on your intended side or the other side of the target line; and where the strike mark appeared on the face.</li>
<li>Change only the loft setting. Do not deliberately alter tee height, target, ball position, or swing intent.</li>
<li>Hit another five shots and record the same three items.</li>
<li>Compare the two groups rather than the single longest shot. Favor the setting that creates the more repeatable launch window and the tighter cluster of useful strikes and finishes. If neither group is clearly better, return to the original setting and bring the notes to a fitting session.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> You finish with a written, observable comparison between two settings and can identify one setting that repeated your preferred flight more often, or recognize that the result is inconclusive.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- Choose driver loft for a repeatable course shot, not for a universal recommended number.
- Compare only two nearby settings at a time while holding tee height, target, ball, and routine steady.
- Track launch, strike location, and finish together because one striking drive is not a reliable verdict.
- An inconclusive short test is a reason to seek an individual fitting, not to keep making random changes.
- For competition use, ensure the final club conforms to the applicable equipment rules.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there one best driver loft for every golfer?

No. The useful choice is the loft that supports a golfer’s individual, repeatable launch, strike pattern, and intended ball flight. The PGA of America recommends evaluating equipment choices through fitting for the individual golfer.

### How many balls should I hit when comparing loft settings?

A small, controlled sample can provide a practical clue when time is limited. Five shots per setting is enough for a quick comparison of obvious patterns, but it is not a substitute for an individual fitting when the result is unclear.

### Should I change tee height when I change driver loft?

Not during the first comparison. Keep tee height fixed so the loft setting is the only planned change. You can explore tee height separately after you have recorded a baseline.

### What if one loft produces my longest drive but not my best group?

Favor the setting that more often produces the launch and finish you can use on the course. A single long drive is less useful than a repeatable pattern when choosing a starting point for fitting.

### Can I use an adjustable driver in competition?

Equipment used in competition must conform to the applicable club rules. Confirm the club’s conformity and the applicable competition requirements before play.
