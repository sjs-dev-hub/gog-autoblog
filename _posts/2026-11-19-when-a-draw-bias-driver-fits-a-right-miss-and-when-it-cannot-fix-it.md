## The quick verdict

A draw-bias driver is worth considering only after you can describe the right miss clearly: where the ball starts, how it curves, and where it contacts the face. It is a fitting choice, not a universal cure for every shot that finishes right.

## A right finish is a result, not yet a diagnosis

The familiar scene is a tee shot that peels into the trees on the right while the group waits on the next tee. It is tempting to call that shot a slice and shop immediately for draw bias. Pause first. A ball can finish right after starting right, after starting near the target and curving right, or after beginning left and curving back through the fairway. Those are visibly different patterns, and they should not all lead to the same equipment decision.

Start with what you can observe without guessing about your swing: starting line, curve, strike feel, and where the ball finishes. A single right miss after a hurried swing tells you little. A pattern that survives ten purposeful shots on more than one day is much more useful. Club fitting should account for the individual golfer and measured ball flight, according to the PGA of America’s fitting guidance (https://www.pga.com/story/club-fitting-what-you-need-to-know).

**Guild recommendation:** Before comparing driver models, write down your typical start direction and curve for at least two short range sessions.

## Sort the four clues before blaming the driver

Face, path, strike, and setup are four separate clues rather than four names for the same flaw. For this decision, you do not need to assign a technical cause to each shot. You only need to avoid combining unlike misses into one vague complaint.

Face clue: note where the ball starts relative to your target. Path clue: note the direction of the curve after it starts. Strike clue: note whether contact is regularly in one area or wanders around the face. Setup clue: use a repeatable target line and toe line so that an apparent right miss is not simply the product of aiming somewhere other than you intended. If your notes show wide variation in all four, equipment changes are operating on a moving target.

A golfer whose ball repeatedly starts near the target then curves right has a different conversation to bring to a fitter than the golfer whose ball starts right with little curve. The key is not to self-diagnose from one launch-monitor number or one memorable drive. Bring the visible pattern and let measured ball flight inform the fitting process, as the PGA of America recommends (https://www.pga.com/story/club-fitting-what-you-need-to-know).

**Guild recommendation:** Use the same target and alignment reference during your observation sessions, then describe the pattern in plain language rather than technical swing terms.

## What a draw-bias conversation should sound like

A productive fitting conversation begins with a constraint: “My usual shot starts right and curves farther right,” or “My contact is centered but my stock tee shot keeps finishing right.” That gives the fitter something concrete to measure against your current driver. It is more valuable than asking for “the most forgiving draw driver,” because forgiveness alone does not identify the flight pattern you want to manage.

Bring your current driver if possible, and compare ball flight rather than making a decision from appearance, sound, or a label on the sole. The PGA of America states that fitting should account for the individual golfer and measured ball flight (https://www.pga.com/story/club-fitting-what-you-need-to-know). That principle matters especially here: a draw-bias designation is a starting hypothesis for a golfer with a recurring right miss, not proof that the club will improve every right miss.

If you play in events governed by the Rules of Golf, use conforming equipment. The USGA equipment rules apply to adjustable and non-adjustable driver designs (https://www.usga.org/equipment-standards/equipment-rules-2019/equipment-rules.html).

**Guild recommendation:** Ask the fitter to compare your current driver and any candidate using the same target, repeated shots, and recorded ball flight.

## Who should wait before changing drivers

The golfer most likely to waste money is the one chasing a different head after every round. If the miss alternates between a high right curve, a low pull, and a heel-side weak shot, the immediate need is a clearer baseline. A draw-bias driver may still be appropriate later, but there is no stable pattern yet to match.

Likewise, check the unglamorous details before treating the driver as the answer. Aiming farther right to make room for a miss can make a rightward finish look worse. Changing ball position from one tee shot to the next removes a useful comparison. Trying to swing harder whenever the fairway tightens can turn a normal pattern into an outlier. None of these observations diagnose a swing; they simply make your equipment evaluation more honest.

The best outcome may be confidence that your current driver is not the first variable to change. That is still progress, because it prevents a club purchase from becoming a substitute for information.

**Guild recommendation:** Delay the purchase if you cannot reproduce the same broad ball-flight pattern across two sessions with a consistent setup.

## Compare only after you know what to compare

Once you have a stable description of the miss, compare candidate drivers in a fitting context and judge the result against a practical on-course standard: Does the repeated pattern move closer to your intended target area while contact remains manageable? Avoid judging a candidate by one dramatic draw or one unusually long shot. The useful comparison is the collection of ordinary swings you are likely to make on the 14th tee with a narrow landing area and a scorecard already carrying consequences.

There is also a preference tradeoff. Adjustable and non-adjustable designs both fall under the applicable equipment rules when they conform (https://www.usga.org/equipment-standards/equipment-rules-2019/equipment-rules.html). An adjustable design may suit a golfer who wants a fitter-led comparison of settings; a non-adjustable design may suit a golfer who values simplicity. Neither preference replaces measured ball flight or a consistent shot pattern.

## Ten-Ball Right-Miss Map

Use a driving range with a clearly visible target, alignment sticks or two clubs laid on the ground, ten balls, and a way to mark results in a phone note. Place one stick parallel to the intended target line outside the ball and one across the toe line to help make your address position repeatable. Do not place either stick where it could be struck.

<ol>

<li>Choose one target and make five practice swings while checking that your feet, hips, and shoulders are arranged consistently relative to the ground sticks.</li>
<li>Hit ten tee shots at a comfortable, controlled effort rather than trying to produce maximum distance.</li>
<li>After every shot, record four observations: whether it started left, near, or right of the target; whether it curved left, straight, or right; whether contact felt centered, heel-sided, toe-sided, high, or low; and whether the result was similar to the previous shot.</li>
<li>Review the notes only after all ten shots. Group the shots by their starting line and curve before looking for any connection between contact feel and the miss.</li>
<li>Repeat the session on another day before treating the pattern as a reason to shop for a different driver.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> The useful result is not a perfect pattern. Success is being able to state a repeatable pattern in one sentence, such as, “Most of my shots start right and continue right,” while also knowing whether contact feels consistent enough to discuss that pattern in a fitting.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- Do not classify every ball that finishes right as the same miss.
- Record starting line, curve, contact feel, and setup consistency before shopping.
- A draw-bias driver is a fitting option for a repeatable pattern, not a diagnosis or guaranteed correction.
- Use a qualified fitting that considers the individual golfer and measured ball flight.
- Choose between adjustable and non-adjustable designs based on the fitting result and your preference for simplicity or fitter-led setting comparison.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should every right-handed golfer who slices buy a draw-bias driver?

No. First establish whether the ball starts right, curves right, or does both, and whether contact is consistent. A draw-bias driver is best treated as a fitting option for a repeatable pattern rather than an automatic answer to every right finish.

### Can I tell whether a draw-bias driver suits me from one range session?

One session can produce a useful clue, but a repeated pattern across more than one session is a stronger basis for a fitting decision. The PGA of America advises that fitting account for the individual golfer and measured ball flight (https://www.pga.com/story/club-fitting-what-you-need-to-know).

### Should I choose an adjustable or non-adjustable driver?

Choose based on the fitting result and the amount of simplicity you want in use. Both adjustable and non-adjustable driver designs are subject to the applicable USGA equipment rules when they conform (https://www.usga.org/equipment-standards/equipment-rules-2019/equipment-rules.html).

### What should I bring to a driver fitting?

Bring your current driver if possible, plus a plain-language description of your usual starting line, curve, contact pattern, and on-course situation. That gives the fitter a useful baseline for comparing measured ball flight.
