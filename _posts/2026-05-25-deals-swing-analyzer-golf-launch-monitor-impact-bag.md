When one tee shot climbs and falls short while the next starts low and runs, the tempting fix is to copy a friend’s loft. A better move is to identify the repeatable shot problem, hold the rest of the test as steady as possible, and choose only the loft setting that improves the result you can actually observe.

Choose driver loft to address a repeatable launch-and-flight problem, not because a particular number is supposed to suit every golfer.

## Start with the tee shot that costs you strokes.

The decision often arrives after a familiar hole: a driver launches low into a headwind and never carries the bunker, or it rises high enough to look promising but finishes in the same short area again and again. Do not begin by asking whether 9, 10.5, or 12 degrees is “right.” Begin with one observable problem. Is the usual solid strike leaving in a flight window that is too low for the carry you need? Is it climbing higher than you want? Or is there no stable pattern because contact and start direction change on nearly every swing? Those are three different situations, and only the first two are clean loft questions.

**Guild recommendation:** Write down one sentence before testing, such as: “My ordinary solid drives start lower than the carry window I need,” or “My ordinary solid drives repeatedly climb above my intended window.”

## Separate launch, strike, and final ball flight.

These observations are easy to blend together, which is why loft changes can be judged unfairly. Launch is the initial window the ball leaves through. Strike is the face contact you can observe with face marks or impact tape. Ball flight is the full result: start, height, curve, landing, and rollout. A shot struck noticeably high or low on the face is not a clean verdict on loft, because it is not comparable to a centered strike. Likewise, a drive that starts in an unintended direction does not tell you much about whether the height was useful. First compare shots with broadly similar contact; then judge whether the flight improved.

**Guild recommendation:** Discard the obvious outliers before comparing lofts. A smaller group of comparable shots is more useful than a larger group of mixed contact.

## Make one variable earn its place.

For a golfer whose ordinary, similar strikes consistently fly below the intended window, the next comparison can be a higher loft setting or head. The outcome to look for is not a dramatic single shot; it is a more repeatable usable flight across several ordinary swings. For a golfer whose comparable strikes repeatedly climb above the intended window, test a lower option and look for a controlled flight that still carries the required trouble. If the same loft produces low bullets one swing and towering shots the next, resist treating the number as the answer. The immediate constraint is inconsistent comparison, not insufficient adjustment.

**Guild recommendation:** Change loft first and keep tee height, ball model, target, and swing intention fixed. Changing several inputs at once leaves no trustworthy explanation for the result.

## Use the hole, not the ego, to define “better.”

A better flight is the one that solves the actual tee-shot task. On a hole with a carry over a hazard, the useful comparison is whether a normal strike clears that carry with room to spare. On a firm, open hole, a flatter flight that finishes in a playable area may be perfectly useful. A golfer who drives into a crosswind should also judge where the ball finishes relative to the target, not height alone. This is why a universal loft recommendation is so fragile: two golfers can need different flights on the same day, and one golfer can need different tee-shot outcomes on different holes.

**Guild recommendation:** Choose a target window and a practical finish area before you hit. “Higher” or “lower” is not a verdict until it is connected to a real on-course requirement.

## Know when a fitting is the efficient decision.

A self-test can narrow the question, but it cannot replace an individual evaluation when the signals conflict. PGA of America guidance describes club fitting as a way to evaluate equipment choices for the individual golfer. Bring your observed pattern to that conversation: for example, that comparable strikes in one setting repeatedly flew below a carry requirement, or that a second setting produced a tighter usable window. That is more actionable than arriving with a preferred loft number. If you play in competition, also ensure that the equipment you use conforms to the applicable club rules under the USGA Equipment Rules.

**Guild recommendation:** Seek a fitting if two settings trade one problem for another, if contact is too inconsistent to compare, or if you need confidence that your competition equipment conforms.

## The Three-Flight Loft Comparison

Use one driver head and one ball model on a range or fitting bay. Pick one distant target, tee the ball at the same height for every shot, and test only two loft positions or two lofted heads. If possible, use the same shaft and avoid changing your grip, ball position, or swing intention during the comparison.

<ol>

<li>Warm up, then hit five shots that are merely playable rather than trying to produce maximum speed.</li>
<li>With the first loft, hit six drives toward the same target. After each shot, note only three things: where the ball struck the face if you can see it, its starting window, and whether it finished lower, medium, or higher than your intended window.</li>
<li>Switch to the second loft and repeat the same six-shot set with the same tee height and target.</li>
<li>Set aside obvious mishits. Compare the remaining shots as groups, asking which loft produced the most repeatable starting window and the most useful carry-and-roll result for the space in front of you.</li>
<li>If neither group has a clear pattern, stop rather than chase a conclusion. Repeat on another day or book a fitting comparison.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> You have a useful result when one option produces a visibly tighter cluster of ordinary, solidly struck shots in the flight window you intended, without requiring you to make a different swing.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- There is no universal driver-loft answer; begin with a repeated on-course flight problem.
- Compare launch, face strike, and full ball flight as separate observations.
- Test one loft change at a time, using the same ball, tee height, target, and swing intention.
- Ignore obvious mishits when judging a loft change, but do not ignore a pattern of inconsistent strike.
- An individual fitting is the sensible next step when your comparison does not reveal a stable pattern.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I choose a higher driver loft when I want more carry?

Treat that as a comparison to test, not a rule to accept. With similar strike, tee height, target, and swing intention, see whether the higher option creates a more repeatable flight that clears the carry requirement you actually face.

### Can face strike make me choose the wrong loft?

Yes. If one group of shots is struck in visibly different places on the face from another, you are comparing more than loft. Group broadly similar strikes first, then evaluate the flight pattern.

### How many shots should I use to compare lofts?

Use enough ordinary shots to see a pattern rather than judging a single best drive. A six-shot group for each setting is a practical starting point, provided you set aside obvious mishits and keep the conditions consistent.

### When should I book a driver fitting?

Book a fitting when different lofts produce mixed signals, when you cannot make comparable contact, or when you want equipment evaluated for your individual game. PGA of America guidance supports individual evaluation through fitting.

### Does my competition driver need to conform to rules?

Yes. The USGA states that equipment used in competition must conform to the applicable club rules.
