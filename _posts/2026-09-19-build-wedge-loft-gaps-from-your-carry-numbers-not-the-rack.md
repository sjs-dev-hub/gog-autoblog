## The familiar problem is not always a missing club.

You have 85 yards left, and the lower-lofted wedge asks for a soft, uncertain swing while the next wedge asks for a hard one. That tension is useful information, but it does not yet prove that another wedge belongs in the bag. First separate a true distance hole from a confidence hole. A true hole remains when you make normal, repeatable swings with adjacent clubs and neither produces the intended carry. A confidence hole may disappear when you learn that a slightly shorter version of a familiar swing covers the shot well enough.

**Guild recommendation:** Do not shop until you can state the missing shot in plain terms: the approximate carry window, the clubs on either side of it, and the swing you currently dislike making.

## Start at the pitching wedge and map downward.

Your pitching wedge is the anchor because it is the club immediately above the specialty-wedge end of the set. Record the carry distance you actually see from it, then do the same with each existing wedge. Put the clubs in loft order and compare their observed landing areas. The goal is not to force every interval to look identical on paper. The goal is to avoid two clubs that repeatedly cover the same shot while leaving another scoring distance exposed. A golfer whose pitching wedge and next wedge overlap has a different problem from a golfer whose next available club leaves a large uncovered window.

**Guild recommendation:** Build the proposed sequence from observed carry results: pitching wedge, current wedge or wedges, then only the candidate loft that fills the clearest space.

## Choose a wedge job before you choose a wedge name.

“Gap,” “sand,” and “lob” are useful shopping labels, but the bag needs jobs rather than labels. The next wedge might be the one that makes a comfortable approach possible from a recurring yardage. It might instead be the club that gives you a higher, shorter-carry option near the green. Those are different decisions. If the issue occurs on ordinary approach shots, prioritize a distinct stock-swing carry. If the issue occurs almost entirely around the green, consider whether technique and shot selection with a current wedge answer the need before adding a club that may rarely be used on full shots.

**Guild recommendation:** Write a one-line job description for the candidate wedge, such as “covers the carry between my pitching wedge and current highest-lofted wedge,” and reject options that do not improve that job.

## Treat partial swings as part of the system, not an inconvenience.

A wedge set does not need a separate club for every number. If a deliberate, repeatable shorter swing with one wedge lands in the missing window, a new wedge may create redundancy instead of simplicity. Conversely, if the only solution requires you to change tempo, manipulate the face, or guess at effort, the gap may be meaningful. The practical standard is not perfection. It is whether you have a shot you can choose under ordinary playing pressure without feeling that you must invent it over the ball.

**Guild recommendation:** Keep an existing setup when a controlled swing produces the needed carry reliably; consider another loft when adjacent clubs require opposite extremes of effort for the same common shot.

## Use fitting to check the whole set, not just the prospective wedge.

A club fitting evaluates the golfer and the complete equipment setup, according to the PGA of America. That makes a fitting especially helpful when your pitching wedge loft, current wedge lofts, or measured carries do not tell a clear story. Bring the carry notes from your practice plan and explain the one shot you are trying to improve. A complete-set view can prevent the narrow mistake of selecting a wedge in isolation and discovering that it overlaps another club.

**Guild recommendation:** If your recorded distances vary enough that the missing window is unclear, ask a qualified fitter to assess the complete setup rather than requesting a specific wedge label.

## Keep the final choice playable and conforming.

Before placing a new club in play, check that it conforms to the applicable equipment rules. The USGA equipment rules govern club design and conformance. For the golfer, the sensible takeaway is simple: retain the loft map, confirm what you are adding, and verify conformance if you play in competitions where the rules apply. A well-spaced set is useful only if you understand what each club is for and can put it in the bag with confidence.

**Guild recommendation:** Save a short wedge chart with each club’s loft marking, observed stock carry, and intended job, then revisit it after practice or a few rounds.

## Map the Wedge Window Before You Shop.

Use a practice area or launch monitor where you can observe carry distance safely. Bring your pitching wedge and every current wedge, 18 to 24 balls, and a notebook or phone for notes.

<ol>

<li>Warm up normally, then choose one repeatable stock swing that you can make without trying to hit at maximum effort.</li>
<li>Hit six shots with each club using that same stock intention, ignoring obvious mishits rather than trying to rescue the average.</li>
<li>Write down the carry result you observe for the solid shots and note where two neighboring clubs produce nearly the same landing area.</li>
<li>Circle the largest useful space between adjacent clubs, then name the on-course shot it would improve, such as an approach that currently requires an uncomfortable in-between swing.</li>
<li>Before selecting a loft, repeat the comparison with one controlled shorter swing to see whether your current clubs already cover the missing area.</li>

</ol>

## The practical takeaway

- Measure observed carry before treating an awkward yardage as evidence that you need another wedge.
- Anchor the loft map at the pitching wedge and look for a genuinely uncovered landing window below it.
- Buy a wedge for a defined on-course job, not because a category name sounds like it should be in every bag.
- A trusted controlled swing can be a better solution than adding a club that overlaps your current set.
- Use a complete-set fitting when your numbers do not reveal a clear answer, and verify conformance where relevant. 

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the first number I should collect before buying a wedge?

Start with the carry distance you observe from your pitching wedge on a normal, repeatable stock swing, then record the same information for every wedge already in the bag.

### Should every wedge have exactly the same distance interval?

Not necessarily. The useful test is whether each club has a distinct, playable carry job and whether a common scoring window remains uncovered.

### Can a partial swing replace another wedge?

Yes, when that shorter swing is repeatable and comfortable enough for the shot you face. It is less persuasive as a solution when it requires a guess or an extreme change in effort.

### When is a fitting worth considering for wedges?

A fitting is particularly useful when your measured carries do not make the gap clear or when you want the prospective wedge considered alongside the complete equipment setup.

### Do I need to consider equipment rules when adding a wedge?

If you play where equipment conformance matters, check the applicable rules before putting the club in play, because club design and conformance are governed by the equipment rules.
