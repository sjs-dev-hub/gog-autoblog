A low price can disappear quickly if the loft, shaft, condition, or return terms are unclear. Treat every used-driver listing as a set of details to verify, then buy only when those details match the club you can actually use.

## The quick verdict

Buy a used driver online when the listing clearly identifies the exact head, loft, shaft, length or modifications if known, condition, included accessories, and return terms, with photos that support the description. Walk away when important details are missing, conflicting, or cannot be clarified before payment.

## Start with the club you already understand

The most expensive online mistake is not buying an older model. It is buying a club whose basic build bears no useful relationship to the driver you can play. Before browsing, make a short benchmark from your current driver: its marked loft, the wording on its shaft label, its measured length, and the ball-flight problem you are trying to avoid. A golfer who loses too many tee shots right, for example, should not assume that any discounted driver will solve that pattern. The practical goal is narrower: avoid a listing with an unknown or clearly unsuitable setup.

**Guild recommendation:** Write your non-negotiables before opening listings, and separate them from preferences such as finish wear or included headcover.

## Read the listing as a verification task, not a sales pitch

A usable listing lets you identify what is for sale without filling in blanks yourself. Look for the exact driver head or model description, its marked loft, the shaft model and flex marking, handedness, stated length if supplied, and any disclosed changes such as a replacement shaft, altered length, or changed grip. Ask whether the photographs show the same club described in the text. A stock-looking product photo does not establish the condition or configuration of the specific used club being sold. The FTC states that advertising claims should be truthful, non-deceptive, and supported; use that standard as a practical reading habit when a description makes a condition or performance claim.

**Guild recommendation:** Favor listings with specific written details and multiple photographs of the actual club over listings that rely on broad labels such as "excellent" or "plays great."

## Inspect the photos in a fixed order

Begin at the crown, where cosmetic marks can be easy to miss against a glossy finish. Then inspect the face for wear, the sole for scuffs, the hosel area for visible damage, the shaft for marks or splintering, and the grip for obvious wear. This sequence does not tell you how a driver will perform, but it keeps the decision tied to visible evidence. Zoomed images from several angles are more useful than one distant picture because they let you compare the seller’s condition description with the club’s actual appearance.

**Guild recommendation:** Request clear close-up photos of any area that is cropped, shadowed, or described vaguely, and do not treat a seller’s omission as proof that the area is clean.

## Treat shaft and length changes as fit questions

A used driver head may be familiar while the shaft and playing length are not. That distinction matters because the club offered is the complete build in the listing, not merely the head name. Compare the shaft label and stated modifications to your benchmark notes. If the seller cannot identify the shaft or disclose a stated length, you cannot make a meaningful like-for-like comparison with your current club. That uncertainty may be acceptable for a low-stakes experiment, but it is a poor foundation for a driver intended to be your regular tee club.

**Guild recommendation:** Choose disclosed configurations that are close to your known baseline unless you have a clear, deliberate reason to try a different setup.

## Check conformance when it matters to your play

If you play in events where club conformance is important, check the driver through the USGA Informational Club Database before committing. The database can be used to check driver conformance information. This step is especially sensible when a listing is incomplete, when the club’s identity is unclear, or when tournament eligibility is part of the value you expect from the purchase. A database check does not replace confirming that the listing is accurately identifying the club shown.

**Guild recommendation:** Match the club’s identifying information as closely as possible to the database entry, and ask the seller for clarification if the listing does not provide enough detail to do so.

## Price the full risk, not just the club

A lower asking price is only a useful saving if the listing gives you a workable path when the club arrives materially different from its description. Read the return terms before purchase, including who bears any return costs and what condition the club must be in for a return. Confirm whether a headcover, wrench, or other accessory is included only when the listing explicitly says so. Keep screenshots of the description, photos, and stated terms, since those are the details against which you will compare the delivered club.

**Guild recommendation:** Make your final decision only after adding the condition evidence, configuration certainty, and return terms to the price comparison.

## Inspect promptly after delivery

When the driver arrives, compare it with the saved listing before taking it to the range. Check that the handedness, head, loft marking, shaft label, visible condition, and included items match what was represented. Photograph the club as received, especially if you see a difference from the listing. This is not an accusation against a seller; it is a disciplined way to determine whether the item delivered matches the decision you made online.

**Guild recommendation:** If a meaningful discrepancy appears, use the seller or marketplace’s stated process promptly and rely on your saved listing evidence.

## Use Your Current Driver as a Listing Benchmark

Bring your current driver, a tape measure or ruler, a phone camera, and a notes app to a flat indoor surface or a practice area. This is a record-keeping exercise, not a swing diagnosis.

<ol>

<li>Photograph the crown, face, sole, shaft label, grip, and any adjustable-hosel markings on your current driver.</li>
<li>Record the loft marking, shaft model and flex marking, and the club’s measured length from the end of the grip to the sole when the club is placed in a consistent measuring position.</li>
<li>Write three simple observations from recent rounds or range sessions, such as whether you commonly start drives left or right, struggle to launch the ball high enough, or find the club difficult to control.</li>
<li>When reading a used listing, compare its disclosed loft, shaft marking, stated length or alterations, and condition photos against your notes before considering its price.</li>
<li>Set aside any listing that does not provide enough information to make that comparison or whose photos and written description do not agree.</li>

</ol>

## The practical takeaway

- Know your current driver’s basic setup before deciding whether a used listing is a sensible match.
- A specific description and clear photos of the actual club are more valuable than an attractive condition label alone.
- An undisclosed shaft or length change turns a familiar driver head into an unknown complete club.
- Use the USGA Informational Club Database when conformance is relevant to the golf you play.
- Return terms and saved listing evidence are part of the purchase, not an afterthought.

## Frequently asked questions

### What details should a used-driver listing always show?

At minimum, look for the actual club’s head or model description, handedness, marked loft, shaft model and flex marking, visible condition, photographs of the club being sold, and the return terms. A stated length and any modifications are also valuable because they help you compare the listing with your current driver.

### Should I buy a used driver with cosmetic wear?

Cosmetic wear can be acceptable when it is clearly shown and accurately described, because you can decide whether it fits your standards and budget. The larger concern is uncertainty: avoid treating unclear photos or incomplete descriptions as harmless cosmetic wear.

### How do I check whether a driver conforms?

Use the USGA Informational Club Database to check driver conformance information, then compare the listing’s identifying details with the relevant database information. If the listing does not identify the club well enough to make that comparison, ask for clarification or choose a better-documented listing.

### What should I do if the delivered driver differs from the listing?

Compare the club against your saved screenshots of the description, photos, and stated terms, then document the difference with clear photographs. Follow the seller or marketplace’s stated return or resolution process promptly.
