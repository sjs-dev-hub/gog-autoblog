## Start with the shot that creates the doubt

The familiar problem is standing over an approach that is too much for one wedge and too little for the next. Many golfers respond by shopping for a loft number immediately. That can work, but it can also produce a new wedge that lives beside an old one and asks the same question in a different form.

Begin with the situation, not the label. Is the awkward shot a full carry from the fairway, a half swing from a tight lie, a bunker shot, or a pitch that must stop quickly? A full-swing distance hole calls for orderly carry spacing. A greenside discomfort may instead call for practice with a current club or a different kind of shot requirement. The distinction keeps the purchase tied to an observable on-course need.

**Guild recommendation:** Write down the one or two shots that repeatedly make you hesitate before deciding that a particular loft is missing.

## Map carries before choosing loft spacing

Loft is a useful organizing tool, but the bag performs in carry distances. Two golfers can own wedges with identical stamped lofts and still see different spacing in actual play. Your task is not to pursue a universal pattern; it is to create a sequence in which each wedge earns a distinct normal-swing role.

Use the Wedge Window Check to find the usable carries of the wedges you have. Pay special attention to the clubs on either side of the suspected gap. If one carries farther than expected, adding a wedge based solely on the printed loft can leave crowding at one end of the set. If the carry gap is genuinely large, the new wedge belongs between those two established windows.

Keep the record honest. A best-ever strike is not a planning number, and neither is a single poor contact. Choose the range of carries you can reasonably expect when the shot matters.

**Guild recommendation:** Choose the new wedge’s loft only after you can name the carry window it must cover between two existing clubs.

## Decide whether the missing tool is a gap, sand, or lob wedge

A gap wedge is most sensible when the uncomfortable shot sits between your pitching wedge and your next-lofted wedge. Its job is straightforward: reduce an approach-distance hole without asking for a forced swing.

A sand wedge choice should be led by the shot you need near bunkers and around the green, while still fitting sensibly into the carry sequence. Do not assume that its name settles its role. If it becomes your most trusted club for a particular bunker or pitch situation, account for that use when checking overlap with the rest of the set.

A lob wedge earns space when you can point to a recurring short-sided, elevated, or limited-landing-area situation where your current highest-lofted wedge does not give you a comfortable solution. It is not automatically the answer to every close-range problem. If the only evidence is that you sometimes leave a short pitch long or short, a focused session with the wedges you own may reveal a controllable shot before you add another club.

**Guild recommendation:** Buy a gap wedge for a documented approach window, a sand wedge for a defined scoring-shot role, or a lob wedge only when it solves a repeatable high-loft problem.

## Protect the rest of the bag from a one-club fix

Every additional wedge takes a place that could belong to another club. That is why the decision should consider the complete setup rather than the isolated gap. PGA of America fitting guidance describes fitting as an evaluation of both the golfer and the complete equipment setup, which is the right frame here. A wedge is sensible when its benefit is clear enough to justify the bag space it occupies.

Before purchasing, lay out the entire short end of your set: pitching wedge, current specialty wedges, and the clubs immediately above them. Ask two plain questions. Which club would I use for a routine shot at each carry window? Which club is now redundant? If you cannot answer the first, there is likely a gap. If two answers feel interchangeable, there may be overlap.

If you play in organized competition, also confirm that any club you intend to use conforms to the applicable equipment rules. The USGA equipment rules govern club design and conformance.

**Guild recommendation:** Treat a new wedge as a complete-bag decision, and confirm both its distinct job and its conformity when relevant to your play.

## Make the final choice simple enough to use under pressure

A sensible wedge setup should make club selection calmer. On a routine approach, you should see a carry, choose a club, and make a normal motion. Around the green, you should know which wedge fits the lie and shot you have practiced. More choices are helpful only when they are meaningfully different.

Once you add a wedge, repeat the carry check and practice three common shots with it: a normal carry, a controlled carry, and a greenside shot you actually face. The goal is not to manufacture every possible yardage. It is to learn where the club belongs so it becomes an option you trust instead of an expensive source of indecision.

**Guild recommendation:** Keep the wedge if it gives you a clearer default choice on a recurring shot; reconsider it if it simply duplicates a familiar club.

## The Wedge Window Check

Use a practice area or launch monitor where you can observe carry distance safely. Bring every wedge you currently play, the same type of golf ball if possible, and a notebook or phone for recording results.

<ol>

<li>Choose a clear target line and make five normal, unhurried swings with each wedge. Record the carry result you observe for each shot rather than trying to chase a perfect number.</li>
<li>For each club, note a usable carry window: the cluster of shots you would trust on the course. Disregard obvious mishits, but do not erase ordinary variation.</li>
<li>Place the wedges in carry order and look for two issues: a large space where no club reaches comfortably, or two clubs whose normal-shot windows substantially cover the same ground.</li>
<li>Next, hit three controlled shots with the longer and shorter wedge around the suspected gap. Note whether either produces a predictable carry without forcing an unusually hard or delicate motion.</li>
<li>Write down one course-like example for the gap, such as a preferred carry to a front pin or a bunker carry with limited room beyond it. Use that shot, not a loft label alone, to decide whether another wedge has a job.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> The check succeeds when you can describe one specific carry window that is uncovered by your present wedges, or when you learn that an existing controlled shot already covers it reliably.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- A wedge gap is a carry-distance and shot-requirement problem before it is a loft-number problem.
- Record usable carry windows for your current wedges before shopping for another one.
- The best new wedge has a specific job that neither neighboring club performs comfortably.
- Consider the whole bag, because a new wedge must justify the space it takes.
- Use practice to distinguish a missing club from an existing shot that needs a clearer plan.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the first number I should check before buying another wedge?

Check the usable carry windows of the two wedges around the distance that troubles you. That reveals whether there is a genuine uncovered space or simply an unfamiliar partial shot.

### Should I choose a wedge by its name?

Use names such as gap, sand, and lob as a starting point, not a final decision. Choose according to the specific carry or greenside shot the club must handle in your set.

### Can I solve a wedge gap without buying a club?

Yes. If a controlled swing with an existing wedge covers the needed carry predictably, you may not need another club. The practice plan helps you check that possibility without guessing.

### Why does the rest of the bag matter when choosing a wedge?

A new wedge occupies a place in the full setup and can create overlap elsewhere. PGA of America fitting guidance frames fitting around the golfer and the complete equipment setup.
