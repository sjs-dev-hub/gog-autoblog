When a full shot sits between two familiar clubs, buying the wedge with the most appealing sole stamp is tempting. Start instead with a small carry-distance map, then use stated loft differences to narrow the choices and make a fitting more productive.

## The quick verdict

Do not choose a gap, sand, or lob wedge by name alone. Record what your current wedges produce on comparable shots, identify the largest repeatable hole, and consider a new stated loft only when it gives that hole a clear job.

## The familiar problem is not always a missing club

You have 95 yards to a tucked flag, one wedge feels like a forced knock-down, and the next asks for more than a comfortable swing. That is a real decision point, but it does not automatically identify a gap wedge, sand wedge, or lob wedge as the answer. First define the problem as a carry window: the distance at which your present choices repeatedly feel unclear.

**Guild recommendation:** Name the troublesome carry window before looking at products.

## Start with the whole wedge end of the bag

Make a short inventory of every club you currently use for scoring shots. Include the stated loft, the carry window you observed, and the shot you normally choose when the ball is in fairway-length turf, rough, or a bunker. A club fitting is intended to evaluate the golfer and the complete equipment setup, not one club in isolation, according to the PGA of America. See https://www.pga.com/story/club-fitting-what-you-need-to-know.

**Guild recommendation:** Bring this inventory to a qualified fitting discussion, especially if changing one wedge would alter the role of two others.

## Use loft as a comparison tool, not a distance promise

List the stated lofts in order and subtract each neighboring number to reveal the stated loft intervals. That simple comparison tells you whether the labels on the soles describe a deliberate sequence or a jump you may want to investigate. It does not replace your carry map: two golfers can have different useful windows with the same stated lofts, so the observable outcome remains the deciding evidence for your own bag.

**Guild recommendation:** Consider a candidate wedge only if its stated loft sits sensibly between the clubs surrounding your documented gap.

## Give every wedge one observable job

A sensible addition has a sentence attached to it: “This is my comfortable option for the carry window between these two current clubs.” That job is stronger than “I need a sand wedge” or “I need more loft,” because it can be checked on the range and on the course. If a prospective wedge merely duplicates a carry window you already cover, it may create a new decision problem rather than solve the old one.

**Guild recommendation:** Choose the club role first, then compare stated lofts that could support that role.

## Do not overlook rules and fitting when the bag changes

Before putting a new club into play, confirm that its design and conformance suit the events in which you intend to use it. The USGA states that club design and conformance are governed by the Equipment Rules. See https://www.usga.org/equipment-standards/equipment-rules-2019/equipment-rules.html. A fitting can also examine the new choice in the context of the complete setup rather than treating loft as the sole variable.

**Guild recommendation:** Use your carry map and current-club inventory as the starting materials for a complete-set fitting evaluation.

## Twenty-Minute Wedge Window Map

Use one target area, one consistent type of ball, and your current wedges. Choose a comfortable, low-risk full or near-full motion that you can repeat without forcing extra speed, and bring a notebook or phone for recording results.

<ol>

<li>Write down each current wedge and its stated loft, if it is marked on the club.</li>
<li>Hit five shots with the same intended motion for each wedge toward the same target area.</li>
<li>Record the carry result you observe for each shot, separating clearly struck shots from obvious mishits rather than trying to make every result fit.</li>
<li>For each wedge, write a practical carry window that represents the cluster of usable shots you saw.</li>
<li>Place the windows in order and circle the largest space between neighboring clubs or the distance that repeatedly leaves you undecided on the course.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> The session succeeds when you can name a specific carry window that lacks a comfortable club choice, or when you can see that your existing wedges already overlap enough that a purchase is not yet the answer.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- An awkward scoring distance is a carry-window problem before it is a wedge-label problem.
- Record usable results from your current wedges before deciding that another stated loft is necessary.
- Compare neighboring stated loft intervals, but let your own observed carry windows determine whether a hole actually exists.
- A new wedge earns a place when it has one clear, observable job between the clubs you already use.
- A complete-set fitting is the appropriate next step when a potential wedge change affects the rest of the bag.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I buy a gap wedge because there is a gap wedge in the name?

No. Start by identifying the carry window that lacks a comfortable choice, then compare stated lofts that could give that window a defined role.

### How many shots should I record before making a wedge decision?

Five comparable shots per current wedge is a practical starting sample for the practice plan. The aim is not a perfect number; it is a clear picture of whether a repeatable hole exists.

### What should I bring to a wedge fitting?

Bring your current wedges, their stated lofts if marked, and a simple record of the carry windows you observed. The PGA of America describes fitting as an evaluation of the golfer and the complete equipment setup.

### Does a stated loft guarantee a particular distance?

No distance guarantee follows from the stated loft alone in this guide. Use the marking to compare the sequence of clubs, then use your observed carry windows to judge the practical result.
