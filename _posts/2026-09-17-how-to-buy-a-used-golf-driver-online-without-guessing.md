## The quick verdict

Buy the listing with the clearest, most complete evidence—not simply the lowest asking price. A driver you can identify, compare against your needs, and return under stated terms is usually the more defensible value.

## Start with the shot you are trying to avoid.

On the course, the bargain can feel expensive when the driver arrives with a loft you did not expect, a shaft that does not resemble the listing, or wear that was never shown. Before opening listings, write down your non-negotiables: handedness, preferred loft range, budget ceiling, and whether you need a headcover or an adjustment tool. If your current driver is playable, use its visible loft and shaft label as reference points rather than chasing a vague promise of more distance.

**Guild recommendation:** Make a short must-have list before searching. A listing that misses one must-have is not a bargain for your game.

## Identify the exact club before judging the condition.

A model name alone is not enough to compare two used drivers. Read the title, description, and photos together for the stated model, loft, handedness, shaft label, and included accessories. Look for agreement between them. If the title says one thing while the visible sole marking or shaft label suggests another, pause and ask the seller to clarify in writing. A useful question is simple: “Can you confirm the loft, shaft label, handedness, and everything included in this sale?”

**Guild recommendation:** Treat a mismatch or an unconfirmed basic detail as a reason to move on unless the seller supplies clear, specific evidence.

## Read photos as evidence, not reassurance.

A distant address photo can show that a club exists; it cannot tell you much about the areas that affect your decision. Prioritize separate, well-lit views of the face, crown, sole, hosel area, shaft, grip, and any included headcover. The face and crown deserve extra attention because they are prominent at address and can be difficult to assess from one angled image. A seller need not provide a perfect photo studio, but a serious listing should let you see the club rather than imagine it.

**Guild recommendation:** Ask for a close photo of any area that is absent, blurry, heavily shadowed, or covered by glare. If that request is ignored, price the uncertainty as unacceptable rather than minor.

## Separate normal use from unanswered risk.

Used equipment can show ordinary cosmetic wear, and that is often part of the value proposition. The decision changes when the description is vague and the photos cannot resolve the issue. Do not label damage from a single image or attempt to diagnose whether a mark will affect performance from afar. Instead, state the observable concern: a mark near the hosel is not clearly shown, the face image is too soft to inspect, or the shaft has an area not pictured. Then ask for a close image and a direct description.

**Guild recommendation:** Buy only what you can reasonably verify. A lower price does not convert missing evidence into known condition.

## Make shaft and loft information do real work.

A driver can be the correct head model and still be a poor choice for your swing if its stated build is far from what you can play comfortably. The listing should identify the shaft label and loft, but that information is a starting point for comparison, not a guarantee of a fitting result. A golfer who tends to fight an overly demanding club should not assume any unfamiliar shaft will behave like the one in a favorite demo. Likewise, an adjustable head is only useful if you understand what is included and can set it appropriately after purchase.

**Guild recommendation:** Favor a used driver whose stated loft and shaft information are close to a known workable baseline. Skip unexplained modifications and unlabeled shafts when fit is your main concern.

## Check conformance and seller claims before you pay.

If you play in events where conformance matters, use the USGA Informational Club Database to check driver conformance information for the club you are considering. This is particularly worthwhile when a listing’s identity is unclear or the club is presented in a way that makes verification difficult. Also read the seller’s wording closely. The FTC states that advertising claims should be truthful, non-deceptive, and supported; clear claims about condition, included items, and return terms give you something concrete to compare with the listing photos and messages.

**Guild recommendation:** Save screenshots of the description, photos, and seller answers before purchase. They create a record of what was represented if the delivered club differs from the listing.

## Treat return terms as part of the club’s value.

The moment the package arrives, an online purchase becomes a condition-checking exercise. Before buying, locate the stated return window, who pays return shipping if the item differs from the description, and whether the seller imposes conditions that make a return impractical. These details do not make a club better, but they define the cost of being wrong. A slightly higher-priced listing with clear terms may be easier to live with than a cheaper one that leaves every problem to the buyer.

**Guild recommendation:** Do not complete the purchase until you can state the return terms in one sentence. If you cannot find them, ask before paying.

## Use a final five-question gate.

When the listing is tempting, reduce the decision to five questions: Can I identify the exact club? Can I see the relevant areas clearly? Does the stated loft and shaft fit my known needs? Are the included items and return terms explicit? Has the seller answered the missing-detail question directly? Four yes answers and one unresolved issue still leave you buying uncertainty. The discipline to wait is often the saving.

**Guild recommendation:** Proceed only when all five answers are yes. A saved search costs nothing, while a disappointing delivery can consume both money and practice time.

## The Three-Listing Comparison Drill.

Open three used-driver listings that appear to fit your budget. Create three columns on paper labeled identity, condition, and fit, then keep your current driver nearby if you know its loft or shaft details.

<ol>

<li>Under identity, record the stated model, loft, handedness, shaft label, and included headcover or adjustment tool for each listing. Mark any item that is not visible or not stated.</li>
<li>Under condition, inspect the available photos for the face, crown, sole, hosel area, shaft, grip, and headcover. Write only what the photo shows; use “not shown” rather than guessing.</li>
<li>Under fit, compare each listing’s stated loft and shaft information with what you currently play or with the characteristics you already know suit you. Do not treat an unfamiliar shaft label as interchangeable with your current one.</li>
<li>Send the same short question to the seller of any leading listing: ask for a close photo of the missing area and confirmation of the stated loft, shaft, and return terms.</li>
<li>Choose only a listing that has no unanswered deal-breaking question. If none clears that bar, save your notes and wait for a better-documented option.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> You can explain why one listing fits your requirements using visible evidence and written seller confirmation, rather than a hunch based on the headline price.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- Build your search around known fit needs, not a low headline price.
- Require agreement among the title, description, photos, and seller’s written answers.
- Mark unseen or unclear areas as unknown instead of deciding they are harmless.
- Use the USGA database when driver conformance is relevant to the golf you play.
- Read return terms before payment and preserve the listing record.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I buy a used driver if the listing has only one photo?

Usually, no. One photo rarely lets you confirm identity, visible condition across the club, and included items. Ask for the specific views you need, and pass if the seller cannot provide them.

### What should I ask a used-driver seller?

Ask the seller to confirm the model, loft, handedness, shaft label, included accessories, any condition issue not fully visible, and the return terms. Keep the question specific enough that the answer can be checked against the listing.

### Can I tell whether a used driver will fit me from the listing?

You can compare the stated loft and shaft information with a known baseline, but a listing cannot guarantee a fitting result. Favor familiar, clearly identified specifications over unexplained or missing information.

### Do I need to check driver conformance?

Check it when you play in competitions or situations where conformance matters to you. The USGA Informational Club Database can be used to check driver conformance information.
