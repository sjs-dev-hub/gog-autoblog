## Start with the problem the listing must solve

A used driver is not a bargain simply because its headline price is lower than another listing. It has to be a club you can identify, a condition you can evaluate from the available evidence, and a configuration you can plausibly use. Before comparing listings, write down your handedness, the loft range you are considering, and the shaft label you are prepared to buy. That short list stops a polished photo from pulling you toward a club that does not meet your own constraints.

**Guild recommendation:** Treat a listing as a candidate only after its handedness, loft, and shaft label are stated or visibly shown.

## Confirm the exact driver before judging the condition

Model names can be similar, and a seller’s headline may not tell the full story. Look for clear photographs of the head, sole, hosel area, shaft label, and any included headcover. Compare the visible markings across the listing rather than relying on a broad title alone. If the seller provides enough identification to do so, use the USGA Informational Club Database to check driver conformance information at https://www.usga.org/InfoClubsDB/index.html. That check addresses conformance information; it does not replace a condition review or establish that the particular used club is suitable for you.

**Guild recommendation:** Ask for a clear photo of any area needed to identify the club before you make a condition-based decision.

## Read photos like an inspection, not an advertisement

One overhead photo can make a driver look nearly untouched while leaving the striking face, crown edge, sole, and shaft largely unseen. Work through the images one area at a time. On the face, look for what is plainly visible rather than trying to infer a story from a glare or shadow. On the crown and sole, distinguish a clearly pictured mark from an area that is simply not photographed. Check whether the shaft and grip appear in the photos if their condition matters to your decision. If an important area is not shown at a useful angle, it is an unanswered question, not evidence that the area is clean.

**Guild recommendation:** Request close, well-lit photos of the face, crown, sole, shaft, and grip when any of those areas are absent or unclear.

## Separate the head’s identity from the club’s current build

The club in a used listing may not match the configuration you expect from its model name. Read the shaft label shown in the listing, note the stated loft and handedness, and look for disclosures about changes to the shaft, grip, headcover, or other components. Do not translate a vague description into a precise specification for the seller. If the listing says little, ask a direct question: whether the shaft and grip are original to the club, and whether the stated loft is the loft marked on the head. The useful outcome is not perfect certainty; it is knowing exactly what remains unknown before you pay.

**Guild recommendation:** Record only the specifications that are shown or explicitly stated, and leave anything else blank until the seller answers.

## Make the seller’s description earn your trust

The Federal Trade Commission says advertising claims should be truthful, non-deceptive, and supported at https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing. For a used-driver listing, that is a useful standard for your own reading: compare phrases such as “excellent condition” with the photos and the specific details supplied. A broad condition label is less helpful than a description that identifies visible marks, changes, and included items. When the words and pictures do not answer the same question, ask a neutral, specific follow-up rather than filling the gap with optimism.

**Guild recommendation:** Send short questions that can be answered clearly, such as whether a particular mark is cosmetic, whether an item pictured is included, and whether the listing photos show the actual club for sale.

## Price the uncertainty, not just the driver

Two listings that appear similar can carry different amounts of uncertainty. One may show every relevant angle, list the club’s configuration, and explain the purchase terms. Another may have an attractive number but leave you guessing about the face, shaft, or what happens if the club differs from the description. Compare the total amount you would pay, including any stated shipping cost, against the information each listing provides. A small apparent saving is not automatically meaningful when it requires you to accept several unanswered questions.

**Guild recommendation:** Choose the listing that gives you the clearest evidence for the total commitment you are comfortable making.

## Check the exit path before you commit

Read the stated return and shipping terms before treating a used driver as a final choice. The key question is practical: if the club arrives materially different from the listing, do the stated terms give you a path you understand? Save the listing description and photos you relied on, along with any seller answers, so you can compare the delivered club with the information available at purchase. This is not an attempt to predict a problem; it is a disciplined way to make an online decision with a record of what was represented.

**Guild recommendation:** Do not buy until you can state the return terms, the delivery cost you are accepting, and the evidence you would use to compare the delivered club with the listing.

## The Three-Listing Decision Drill

Open three used-driver listings for clubs you would realistically consider, then make a note with four headings: identity, condition, specifications, and terms.

<ol>

<li>Under identity, write the stated model, loft, handedness, and any visible identifying marks for each listing.</li>
<li>Under condition, note what the photos actually show on the face, crown, sole, shaft, grip, and headcover area, leaving a question mark where an image is missing or unclear.</li>
<li>Under specifications, record the shaft label, length if stated, and any disclosed changes rather than assuming they match the original club.</li>
<li>Under terms, record the seller’s stated return option, shipping cost if shown, and the exact questions you would need answered before purchasing.</li>
<li>Choose the listing with the fewest unanswered questions, not automatically the lowest asking price.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> You can explain in one sentence why your preferred listing has less unresolved risk than the other two.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- Identify the exact head and its stated configuration before you compare condition or value.
- Missing photos and vague specifications are unanswered questions, not reassuring evidence.
- Use the USGA Informational Club Database for conformance information when the listing identifies the driver clearly enough.
- Compare seller claims with photographs and specific disclosures, using the FTC’s truthfulness standard as a sensible reading habit.
- Pay for the level of certainty you need, and understand the stated return terms before committing.

## Frequently asked questions

### What photos should I request for a used driver?

Request clear, well-lit photos of the face, crown, sole, shaft label, grip, and any area that is obscured, shadowed, or mentioned in the description.

### Can I verify whether a used driver is conforming?

You can use the USGA Informational Club Database to check driver conformance information when you can identify the club clearly enough, but that does not evaluate the condition of the particular used club.

### Should I trust a seller’s condition grade?

Use a condition grade as a starting point, then compare it with the listing photos and the seller’s specific description of marks, changes, and included items.

### What is the most important question to ask a seller?

Ask the question that removes the largest uncertainty for your own decision, such as whether the pictured shaft is included, whether a visible mark is on the actual club, or what the stated return terms cover.
