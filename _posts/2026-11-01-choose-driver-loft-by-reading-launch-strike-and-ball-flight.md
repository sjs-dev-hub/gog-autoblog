The frustrating drive is not always the one that misses the fairway; often it is the centered-looking shot that launches into an unfamiliar window and leaves you unsure whether to change loft, tee height, or nothing at all. Use launch, strike location, and ball flight together to narrow the choice before confirming it in an individual fitting.

## The quick verdict

Choose driver loft from repeated shots, not from another golfer’s stated loft: compare candidate settings only when strike pattern, starting line, and intended shot are reasonably consistent.

## Start with the drive you are trying to keep

A player who needs a tee shot to carry a front bunker has a different decision from a player whose priority is keeping the ball below a crosswind. Before looking at a loft number, name the shot you want to repeat: its starting window, general curve, and landing area. That target gives meaning to the observations that follow, while a loft stamped on the sole does not.

**Guild recommendation:** Write one sentence describing your preferred driver flight and the hole situation where it matters most.

## Read launch, strike, and flight as one picture

Launch is the window in which the ball leaves the clubface. Strike is the place on the face where contact occurred. Ball flight is the whole visible result, including where the shot starts, how it curves, and how it finishes. None should make the decision alone. For example, a drive that starts in a useful window but comes from scattered face contact is not a clean reason to settle on a loft; the next comparison needs more repeatable contact first.

**Guild recommendation:** For each comparison shot, record one launch description, one strike location, and one simple flight outcome such as usable, too low for the target, or too high for the intended window.

## Avoid changing two explanations at once

It is tempting to lower the loft after seeing a high drive, then also tee the ball lower and swing differently on the next one. That sequence creates a result but not a useful decision. Hold ball, tee height, target, and basic intent steady while comparing loft candidates. If the flight changes while the strike pattern remains broadly similar, you have a meaningful observation to discuss. If strike moves around substantially, the comparison is still telling you something important: loft is not yet the only variable in view.

**Guild recommendation:** Compare two loft candidates only, using a small set of similar-contact shots rather than alternating settings after every swing.

## Use the ball flight to test a course constraint

A useful driver choice has to survive a real constraint. Imagine a hole with a forced carry, a hole where the landing area narrows, or a breezy hole where a particular flight window feels more manageable. Test the candidate lofts against one constraint at a time. The better option is not automatically the one with the most dramatic flight; it is the one that more often produces the usable shot you identified when your contact is comparable.

**Guild recommendation:** Choose one representative on-course constraint for each comparison, and judge candidates by repeatable usable outcomes rather than the best single drive.

## Finish the process with an individual fitting

The PGA of America notes that equipment choices should be evaluated for the individual golfer through fitting. Your notes can make that conversation more productive: show the fitter which loft candidates you compared, what your strike pattern looked like, and which ball-flight window served your target. If you play competition, also confirm that the club you intend to use conforms to the applicable equipment rules, as required by the USGA Equipment Rules.

**Guild recommendation:** Treat your observations as fitting inputs, then verify the final competition club against the applicable rules.

## The Three-Flight Loft Comparison

Use the same ball model, tee height, target, and warm-up routine for every shot. If you have access to two driver loft options or adjustable settings, use only two candidates and keep every other setting unchanged. Mark the face lightly with a removable impact spray or use another safe method of seeing contact.

<ol>

<li>Hit five drives with the first loft candidate at a target that gives you room to observe the full flight and landing area.</li>
<li>After each shot, note only three items: where the ball met the face, its initial launch window, and whether the finished ball flight was usable for your intended hole.</li>
<li>Hit five drives with the second candidate under the same setup, resisting the urge to change tee height or make a compensating swing adjustment.</li>
<li>Set aside obvious mishits, then compare the remaining shots as groups rather than allowing one unusually good drive to decide the result.</li>
<li>Bring the notes to a club fitter and ask to compare the candidates while preserving the strike and ball-flight pattern you observed.</li>

</ol>

## The practical takeaway

- There is no universal driver-loft answer because the decision should be evaluated for the individual golfer through fitting.
- A loft comparison is more useful when launch, face contact, and finished ball flight are observed together.
- Keep tee height, ball, target, and swing intention consistent so that a loft comparison has a clear purpose.
- Judge candidates against a course constraint and repeated usable outcomes, not one memorable drive.
- Use your comparison notes to guide an individual fitting and confirm competition equipment conformity when relevant.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I choose a driver loft from my handicap or swing-speed category?

Use those labels only as background, not as a final prescription; compare your own repeated launch, strike, and ball-flight pattern, then confirm the choice through an individual fitting.

### What if one loft produces one outstanding drive?

Keep it in perspective and compare groups of similar-contact shots, because one exceptional result does not show whether the flight is repeatable for your intended target.

### Can I use an adjustable driver to make this comparison?

Yes, provided you compare only two candidate settings at a time and hold the ball, tee height, target, and other setup conditions as steady as possible.

### When should I stop trying to solve loft on the range?

Stop when strike pattern or setup changes make the comparison unclear, and take your observations to a qualified fitter for an individual evaluation.
