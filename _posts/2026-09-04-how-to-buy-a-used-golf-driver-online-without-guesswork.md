A used driver listing is worth serious consideration only when its photos, stated specifications, and condition description tell the same clear story. If an important detail is missing, ask for it or move on rather than filling in the gap with hope.

## Start with the exact club, not the headline

A title such as “great driver” is not a usable description. Before weighing condition, establish the exact model, handedness, and stated loft from the listing text and photos. Record the shaft label and flex only when they are clearly shown or explicitly described; do not infer them from a color pattern or a partial image. This protects you from comparing a promising headline with a club configured differently from the one you meant to buy.

**Guild recommendation:** Treat any unverified configuration detail as unknown, not as a favorable assumption.

## Read photos like an inspection, not an advertisement

Photos should let you account for the areas that affect your comfort with the purchase: crown, face, sole, hosel area, shaft, and grip. A single address-style photo may establish that a driver exists, but it cannot settle questions about the surfaces you care about. Zoom in, compare multiple angles, and separate what is visible from what the seller merely says. Clear images do not guarantee a result, but missing images leave you unable to make an informed comparison.

**Guild recommendation:** Prefer the listing that shows the whole club consistently over one with a stronger condition label but incomplete visual evidence.

## Make condition language earn your trust

Words such as “excellent,” “clean,” and “normal wear” are incomplete unless the photos and details support them. The FTC states that advertising claims should be truthful, non-deceptive, and supported. Apply that standard as a buyer: look for a specific description of what is included and what condition is visible, then ask a focused follow-up when the wording is broad. A seller can answer a question about one area of the club far more usefully than they can answer whether it is “in good shape.”

**Guild recommendation:** Ask for one observable fact at a time, such as a close photo of a particular area or confirmation of an included headcover.

## Check whether the stated model is conforming

If playing under equipment rules matters to you, check the stated driver model in the USGA Informational Club Database. The database can be used to check driver conformance information. This is a model-verification step, not a substitute for confirming that the listing actually depicts and describes that model correctly. Save the listing details you used for the search so you can revisit the result if the seller corrects the description.

**Guild recommendation:** Use the USGA database when conformance is important to your rounds, and pause if the listing does not identify the model clearly enough to search.

## Price the uncertainty, not just the club

A lower asking price does not automatically create better value when the listing leaves you guessing about configuration, condition, or what comes with the club. Compare listings using the same written fields: exact club, visible condition evidence, included items, seller answers, and any stated return terms. Then decide whether the cheaper option remains attractive after you account for the unanswered question that could matter most to you. A golfer who needs a particular handedness and loft has less room for compromise than someone browsing for a backup club.

**Guild recommendation:** Choose the listing with the fewest decision-critical unknowns, even when another title looks like the headline bargain.

## Send a short question before making a close call

When two listings are similar, a precise message can reveal which seller is prepared to document the club. Ask for information that can be verified in a reply or a photo: “Could you confirm the stated loft on the head?” or “Could you add a close photo of the face?” Avoid asking a vague question about overall condition, because it invites another vague answer. If the reply does not resolve the uncertainty that prompted your question, keep shopping.

**Guild recommendation:** Do not let a quick purchase decision outrun the one answer you need to compare the club fairly.

## The Three-Listing Comparison Drill

Open three used-driver listings that appear to match your preferred handedness, stated loft, and general budget. Create three columns on paper labeled exact model, configuration, condition evidence, seller answers, and decision.

<ol>

<li>Write down only details shown or explicitly stated in each listing, including the model name, handedness, stated loft, shaft label if shown, grip condition if shown, headcover status if stated, and return terms if stated.</li>
<li>For each listing, inspect every available photo and note whether the clubhead, crown, face, sole, shaft, grip, and hosel area are each visible enough to judge their condition.</li>
<li>Mark any mismatch between photos and wording, such as a condition claim that is not supported by a clear view of the relevant area.</li>
<li>Choose one missing detail that would change your decision and send one specific question to the seller, such as asking for a close photo of the face or confirmation of the stated loft.</li>
<li>Keep only listings for which you can state the exact club configuration and the condition evidence without making an assumption.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> You have one leading listing with no decision-critical unknowns, or you have identified that waiting for a clearer listing is the better outcome.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- Identify the exact model, handedness, stated loft, and shaft information before judging whether a used driver suits your needs.
- Use photos to account for the crown, face, sole, hosel area, shaft, and grip rather than relying on a condition label alone.
- Ask sellers for a specific fact or close photo when one missing detail could change your decision.
- Check a clearly identified model in the USGA Informational Club Database when conformance matters to you.
- Compare uncertainty across listings, because a low price cannot answer a missing question.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should I ask a seller about a used driver?

Ask the question that resolves your largest unknown, such as confirmation of the stated loft, a clear photo of the face, or whether a listed headcover is included. Keep the request specific and observable.

### Can I trust a used-driver condition grade?

Use a condition grade as a starting point, not a conclusion. Compare it with clear photos and specific wording about the areas you need to evaluate.

### How do I check whether a driver is conforming?

Search the stated model in the USGA Informational Club Database. First make sure the listing identifies the model clearly enough for a meaningful search.

### When should I pass on a used driver listing?

Pass when you cannot establish the exact configuration, cannot see the areas that matter to your condition decision, or do not receive a clear answer to a decision-critical question.
