## Begin with the tee shot that costs you strokes

The familiar mistake is standing over a driver shelf and asking whether 9, 10.5, or 12 degrees is “best.” That question has no useful answer until it is attached to a visible problem. A golfer whose solid shots repeatedly fly on a low, flat window has a different decision to make from a golfer whose centered strikes climb steeply and finish short of a familiar landing area. Describe the problem in plain language: low flight, excessive height, an unpredictable window, or a flight that begins and finishes away from the intended line. This makes loft a focused comparison variable, not a cure-all.

**Guild recommendation:** Write one sentence about the recurring shot you want to improve before looking at any driver setting.

## Separate strike evidence from ball-flight evidence

Ball flight is the outcome; strike location adds context. A heel, center, or toe strike is an observable event, not merely a feeling in the hands. If contact marks are scattered across the face, a change in loft can produce a misleading result because you are comparing different strikes as well as different settings. Conversely, when several strikes cluster in a similar area, the resulting flight gives you a cleaner basis for comparing loft options. Do not label a setting good or bad after the one drive that caught the center perfectly.

**Guild recommendation:** Use face-contact tape or a removable marker during comparisons, and judge a group of broadly similar strikes rather than the single longest ball.

## Change loft only after you hold the other conditions still

A useful range session is deliberately unexciting. Keep the same ball, tee height, target, and intended shot. Make a small loft comparison, record what you see, and resist changing your aim or trying to manufacture a different swing between groups. For example, if a golfer is testing a flight that seems too low, the productive question is not whether a higher-numbered loft must solve it. The productive question is whether the alternative produces a more repeatable, playable flight from comparable contact. The same discipline applies to a golfer trying to reduce an overly high flight.

**Guild recommendation:** Compare two options at a time and make every other controllable condition as similar as possible.

## Let the target decide what “better” means

A better driver flight is the one that repeatedly serves the hole in front of you. On a hole with a narrow landing area, a golfer may value a predictable starting line and curve more than an occasional extra-long result. On a hole requiring carry over trouble, the key observation may be whether the flight consistently clears the relevant hazard during normal play. Use a target or a specific landing corridor in practice, because “high” and “low” alone do not tell you whether the shot is useful. Your notes should describe observable outcomes, not a hoped-for distance gain.

**Guild recommendation:** Define success by a target-based outcome, such as a repeatable flight that stays within your chosen landing corridor.

## Finish with an individual fitting and a rules check

The PGA of America advises that equipment choices be evaluated for the individual golfer through fitting. Take your practice notes with you: the setting tested, the contact pattern, the ball-flight description, and the on-course situation that prompted the search. That gives the fitter a specific question to evaluate rather than a vague request for more distance. If you play in competition, also confirm that the club conforms to the applicable rules; the USGA states that equipment used in competition must conform to the applicable club rules.

**Guild recommendation:** Use your observations to guide an individual fitting, then verify competition conformity when that applies to your golf.

## The One-Variable Driver Loft Check

Use a level practice area, one model of golf ball, your normal tee height, and two driver-loft settings or two drivers with different stated lofts. Place a removable face-contact marker or impact tape on the face if you have it, and aim at one clear target.

<ol>

<li>Warm up, then hit five driver shots with your usual setup and first loft option. Record only the starting direction, the general height of the flight, the finishing curve, and the area of the face where each shot struck.</li>
<li>Keep the ball, tee height, target, and intended swing the same. Change only to the second loft option, then hit another five shots and record the same four observations.</li>
<li>Set aside obvious mishits, then compare the remaining shots as groups. Ask which option produced the more repeatable flight toward the target while preserving the contact pattern you want to repeat.</li>
<li>If neither group is consistent enough to compare, stop changing settings. Bring your notes to a club-fitting appointment so the fitting begins with a defined performance question.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> You finish with a clear observation such as, “With centered contact, this loft gave me the more repeatable flight toward my target,” rather than a conclusion based on one unusually good drive.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- A driver-loft number is not a recommendation until it is tied to a repeatable ball-flight problem.
- Strike location must be observed alongside flight, or a loft comparison can become a comparison of mishits.
- Keep tee height, ball, target, and intended swing consistent while testing one loft variable.
- The useful winner is the option that produces the more repeatable target-serving flight from comparable contact.
- An individual fitting is the appropriate final check for an equipment decision.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I choose driver loft from swing speed alone?

Do not reduce the decision to one number. Start with your repeatable strike and ball-flight observations, then use an individual fitting to evaluate the club choice for you.

### What if my driver shots have several different flight patterns?

First document where the ball is striking the face and look for a recurring pattern. If contact is too scattered to create comparable groups, avoid drawing a loft conclusion from that session.

### Can I test loft without a launch monitor?

Yes. Use a consistent target and record starting direction, height, curve, and face-contact location. A launch monitor can add recorded information, but it does not replace a controlled comparison or an individual fitting.

### How many shots should I use when comparing lofts?

Use enough shots to set aside obvious mishits and compare small groups of similar contact. The aim is a repeatable pattern, not a winner chosen from one exceptional drive.
