## The label is not the fitting result.

A forged-versus-hollow-body decision often becomes misleading when a golfer treats the manufacturing label as a promise of a particular shot. The supplied USGA rules make the more useful baseline clear: conforming club requirements apply regardless of marketing category or construction method. That does not tell you which iron belongs in your bag; it tells you not to confuse category language with a rules-based performance ranking. Read the relevant rules at https://www.usga.org/equipment-standards/equipment-rules-2019/equipment-rules.html.

**Guild recommendation:** Use the construction label to organize a shortlist, then judge the actual clubs rather than assigning a result to the label.

## Separate the questions of feel and outcome.

Feel is still a real purchase consideration because it affects whether you want to make a committed swing at a demanding target. Yet a satisfying strike and a useful on-course pattern are separate questions. Picture a golfer facing a 150-yard approach over trouble: the better candidate is not automatically the one that produced the most memorable shot in a bay, but the one whose repeated shots and address look create the most confidence for that task. The PGA of America advises that club fitting and measured results matter when comparing options, which gives this decision a practical order: observe results, then weigh the feel you can repeat. See https://www.pga.com/story/club-fitting-what-you-need-to-know.

**Guild recommendation:** Write down both observations during the comparison: what you saw in the shot pattern and whether the club encouraged a committed, comfortable swing.

## Make the comparison fair enough to teach you something.

Comparing a long iron from one line against a short iron from another, changing balls between turns, or relying on one flush strike turns a fitting into a collection of impressions. Keep the role in the set comparable, hit both candidates in alternating groups, and bring the same decision to each shot. A golfer who normally plays from firm fairways may prioritize a different visual and strike response than one who routinely faces soft turf, but neither golfer needs to settle that question from a construction term alone. The PGA’s fitting guidance supports using measured comparison rather than assumption at https://www.pga.com/story/club-fitting-what-you-need-to-know.

**Guild recommendation:** Ask the fitter to keep the comparison focused on comparable club roles and to help you review a group of shots rather than a highlight reel.

## Let your bag create the final constraint.

An iron is not chosen in isolation. Before settling on either category, define what problem this club must solve in your set: a dependable approach number, a comfortable club at the top of the iron set, or a look that helps you aim without second-guessing. Then ask whether the candidate’s recorded result fits that job alongside the clubs you already trust. This keeps the decision observable and avoids turning construction into a substitute for a bag plan.

**Guild recommendation:** Bring your current distance gaps and the club role you want to replace to the fitting, then make the final choice only after the candidate has a defined job.

## The Two-Iron Comparison Card

Bring two iron candidates in the same loft category when possible, use the same type of ball for every shot, and create a simple card with columns for club, start direction, carry result, strike location, and feel confidence.

<ol>

<li>Warm up with several easy swings before recording any result, then hit five shots with the first candidate at a controlled, normal-course effort.</li>
<li>Record each shot without assigning a cause to the swing: note only the observed start direction, carry result if available, strike location, and whether you would confidently choose that club for a typical approach.</li>
<li>Repeat the same five-shot sequence with the second candidate, alternating clubs for two more rounds so that neither club benefits only from being hit first or last.</li>
<li>Review the card with the fitter and look for a pattern across the full sample rather than selecting the single best strike from either club.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> The useful outcome is a repeatable preference supported by your recorded results and enough confidence that you would choose the club for an ordinary approach shot.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- Forged and hollow-body are useful comparison labels, not stand-alone verdicts on performance.
- Conforming requirements apply regardless of a club’s marketing category or construction method.
- A fitting should combine measured results with the feel confidence you can repeat under an ordinary on-course decision.
- A fair comparison uses comparable club roles, the same ball, alternating sets, and a recorded group of shots.
- Choose the candidate that fills a defined role in your bag rather than the one with the most persuasive label.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a forged label tell me which iron will perform better for me?

No supplied evidence supports using the label as a personal performance verdict. Use it to form a shortlist, then compare the specific candidates through fitting and recorded results.

### Should I ignore feel when comparing irons?

No. Record feel alongside observed shot results, then favor the option that provides a repeatable result and the confidence to make a committed swing.

### Why compare irons in alternating groups?

Alternating groups reduces the chance that your conclusion rests on a single warm-up phase, a single standout strike, or a changing first impression.

### Are forged and hollow-body irons judged under different USGA conformance requirements?

The supplied USGA rules state that conforming club requirements apply regardless of marketing category or construction method. Read the rules at https://www.usga.org/equipment-standards/equipment-rules-2019/equipment-rules.html.
