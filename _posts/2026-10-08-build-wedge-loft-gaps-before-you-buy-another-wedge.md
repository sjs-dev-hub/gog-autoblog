## The familiar mistake: buying loft to solve a shot decision.

You face a short approach from an uncomfortable number, take something off a wedge, and watch the ball finish well short or long. It is tempting to call that a missing wedge. Sometimes it is. Just as often, the bag already contains two clubs that perform nearly the same task, while the real problem is uncertain carry distance, inconsistent strike, or no dependable plan for the in-between swing. The useful question is not, “Which wedge should I buy?” It is, “What shot can I not cover with a comfortable, repeatable choice?”

**Guild recommendation:** Write down the loft stamped on every wedge you carry before comparing any new club.

## Build the map from the bag you actually play.

Start with the entire scoring end of the set, including the pitching wedge that came with your irons if it is in the bag. Put the lofts in order from lowest to highest. Then add plain-language jobs beside them: a fuller approach, a controlled approach, a low runner, a bunker shot, or a higher greenside shot. The labels matter because a loft gap on paper is not automatically a useful shot gap on the course. Two wedges may produce very similar outcomes for you, while a different loft may give you a clearer decision at a recurring distance.

**Guild recommendation:** Do not choose a candidate loft until you can identify both the club below it and the club above it in your current set.

## Look for a missing window, not a perfect formula.

There is no universal loft sequence that guarantees sensible spacing for every golfer. Your delivery, the way you use partial swings, and the roles you ask each wedge to perform all affect whether a new club creates a useful window. A golfer who confidently changes length and speed may cover several approach windows with fewer wedges. Another golfer may prefer a more defined club choice and benefit from an additional option. Either approach is sensible when the result is a clear, repeatable decision rather than a collection of near-duplicates.

**Guild recommendation:** Favor the candidate that gives you a distinct shot choice with an ordinary swing, not the candidate that merely adds the most loft.

## Separate approach gapping from greenside role.

A gap, sand, or lob wedge can be discussed as a loft, but it also needs a job. Consider the golfer who has a comfortable fuller shot with one wedge and a reliable greenside club with another, yet repeatedly faces a distance between them. That golfer should first examine a middle window. By contrast, a golfer whose approach windows are covered but who cannot comfortably produce the needed height or stopping shot around a green is evaluating a greenside role. Those are different problems, and they can point to different choices.

**Guild recommendation:** Describe the troublesome shot in a sentence before you shop, including where the ball lies, what flight you need, and what your present club forces you to do.

## Use fitting to test the complete setup.

A club fitting evaluates the golfer and the complete equipment setup, according to the PGA of America. That is especially relevant when a prospective wedge affects the rest of the bag rather than replacing a clearly defined role. Bring your current wedges, your loft list, and notes from the window check. Ask the fitter to assess whether the proposed club provides a distinct option in the full setup, not simply whether the club looks appealing on its own. Club design and conformance are governed by the Equipment Rules, so golfers who play under the Rules should also ensure the equipment they use conforms.

**Guild recommendation:** Treat a fitting as a way to check the whole set and its shot windows, particularly if your prospective wedge would change how you use more than one current club.

## Buy only when the decision gets easier.

The best result is not a bag with more choices. It is a bag in which the awkward number produces a calmer decision: this club, this normal swing, this intended landing area. Skip the purchase if your notes show that the troublesome shot moves around from day to day or that several current wedges already reach the same window. In that case, the safer investment is time spent learning one controlled swing window with the wedges you own. Add a club when it removes a specific recurring compromise and leaves every wedge with a recognizable role.

**Guild recommendation:** Before checkout, state the new wedge’s one job and name the existing club it will not duplicate.

## The Three-Swing Wedge Window Check

Use one target or landing area on a practice range or short-game area with enough room to see where shots finish. Bring every wedge you currently carry, a small number of balls, and a way to note the club and intended swing.

<ol>

<li>Choose a comfortable, ordinary full swing with each wedge; do not try to create extra speed or a special flight.</li>
<li>Hit three shots with each wedge toward the same general target area, recording only the club and where each ball carried or finished relative to the others.</li>
<li>Next, hit three controlled, shorter swings with the wedge you would normally use for an in-between shot, using one repeatable backswing feel rather than several improvised swings.</li>
<li>Arrange your notes by shot window: the shots you can cover comfortably, the shots that overlap, and the window that repeatedly asks for an awkward effort.</li>
<li>If a clear uncovered window remains after the exercise, take your current loft list and those observations to a qualified fitter or use them to compare candidate lofts.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> You can point to one distinct shot window that is consistently uncovered, or conclude that your present wedges already overlap enough that another club would not solve the problem.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- A wedge gap is useful only when it corresponds to a recurring shot window you cannot cover comfortably.
- Record every current wedge loft, including the pitching wedge, before considering another club.
- Separate an approach-distance problem from a greenside-flight problem because they may require different solutions.
- A fitting can evaluate the golfer and the complete equipment setup rather than isolating one prospective wedge.
- More loft does not automatically mean more options; a distinct role is the goal.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I buy a gap wedge because I have an awkward distance?

Not automatically. First determine whether the awkward distance is a repeatable missing window between your existing clubs or whether it is mainly an uncertain partial swing or inconsistent contact.

### Is there one correct wedge loft progression?

No universal progression is established by the supplied guidance. A sensible progression is one that gives you distinct, comfortable shot choices within your own complete setup.

### Should I include my pitching wedge when checking gapping?

Yes. Include every wedge you carry, including the pitching wedge, because it may be the lower end of the scoring-club sequence you are trying to organize.

### When is a fitting worthwhile for wedges?

A fitting is particularly useful when the proposed wedge may overlap another club or alter several scoring-shot decisions, because fitting evaluates the golfer and the complete equipment setup.
