## The familiar trap: buying a label instead of solving a shot

You are 85 yards out, caught between a smooth swing that comes up short and a firmer one that can race through the back of the green. It is tempting to call that a “gap-wedge problem,” but the useful question is narrower: what shot can you not currently produce often enough to trust? A missing full carry, a bunker shot, and a soft shot from tight grass are different problems. They may lead to different wedge choices, or to no purchase at all.

**Guild recommendation:** Write down the one situation that causes the most indecision before comparing any lofts.

## Build the sequence from actual carries

Put your current wedges in order from lower loft to higher loft and record a normal, controlled carry window for each. Do not build the plan around the rare flushed ball or around a number printed on a club. You are looking for overlap that makes choices easy and for empty space that repeatedly forces you to manufacture speed or take speed off. If two neighboring clubs produce clearly distinct, repeatable windows, they are doing their jobs even if the loft labels do not look perfectly symmetrical.

**Guild recommendation:** Choose a prospective wedge by the carry window it needs to cover between two existing clubs, not by assuming a particular club name belongs in every bag.

## Separate full-shot gapping from green-side versatility

A golfer may need another wedge for two very different reasons. On approaches, the goal is a dependable carry that fits between clubs already in the bag. Around the green, the goal may be to clear a lip, stop the ball sooner, or play from sand without changing the motion dramatically. A very high-lofted choice can make sense when that second job is genuinely missing, but it is not automatically the answer to a 15-yard approach gap. Conversely, a club that fills an approach window may not be the club you reach for from a short-sided lie.

**Guild recommendation:** Assign each wedge a primary job: lower-lofted scoring approaches, a middle carry window, or higher, shorter shots. Avoid buying two clubs that answer the same job while leaving another unanswered.

## Use the rest of the bag as the constraint

Every added wedge displaces something else. Before committing, consider what club would leave the bag and what shot that club presently handles. The sensible choice is the one that removes more on-course doubt than it creates elsewhere. A player who rarely hits a long club into a green may value a tighter wedge sequence. A player who routinely needs that long-club yardage may decide an occasional partial-wedge shot is the better compromise.

**Guild recommendation:** Make the decision as a bag decision, not a wedge-only decision, and name the club or shot you would give up.

## Finish with a fitting when the answer is still unclear

A fitting evaluates the golfer and the complete equipment setup, rather than treating one club in isolation. That makes it a sound next step when your notes show a real gap but do not make the right loft or role obvious. Bring your current wedges and the distance notes from the practice plan. The useful outcome is not a predetermined club type; it is a sequence that fits your actual bag and the shots you intend to play. Club design and conformance are governed by the equipment rules, so confirm that any club you plan to use in a rules-governed setting conforms to the applicable requirements.

**Guild recommendation:** Use a fitting to validate a documented gap and to review the whole set, especially if changing one wedge would alter the rest of your bag.

## The Three-Window Wedge Map

Choose a flat practice area with three safe, visible targets at different approach distances, and bring the wedges currently in your bag, ten to fifteen balls, and a way to note results.

<ol>

<li>Hit five normal, controlled shots with each wedge toward the target that best suits it, allowing every ball to finish before recording only its general carry window and strike quality.</li>
<li>Identify the two neighboring wedges that create the decision you dislike most, then hit five more shots with each using the same calm setup and a committed finish.</li>
<li>Write down the farthest repeatable carry window for the shorter-lofted club and the shortest repeatable carry window for the higher-lofted club, rather than selecting the single best strike from either group.</li>
<li>If the two windows leave a recurring uncovered space, note the approximate missing shot and whether it is mainly an approach carry, a bunker need, or a high, soft green-side requirement.</li>
<li>Before shopping, try one modestly shorter swing with the longer club and one fuller swing with the shorter club to see whether either existing club covers the window without creating a forced motion.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> The session succeeds when you can describe one repeatable missing shot in plain language, or when you discover that an existing wedge covers it with a controlled swing.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- A wedge earns a place by solving a repeatable carry or shot problem.
- Record normal carry windows before using loft labels to guide a purchase.
- Treat approach gapping and green-side shot-making as separate decisions.
- An existing wedge and a controlled swing may cover the space without another club.
- When the answer remains uncertain, evaluate the complete setup through a fitting.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I know whether I need a gap wedge or a lob wedge?

Start with the missing job. If the problem is a repeatable approach carry between two existing wedges, compare options that fill that window. If the problem is producing a higher or shorter green-side shot, evaluate whether a higher-lofted option addresses that specific task.

### Should my wedge loft gaps all be identical?

Not necessarily. The practical test is whether neighboring wedges create useful, repeatable carry windows and distinct shot roles for you. A visually neat sequence is less valuable than one that removes forced swings.

### Can I decide from the loft stamped on my current wedges?

Use the stamped loft as a starting reference, then verify what each club actually does in your hands. Your notes should focus on normal, controlled carries and the situations where you hesitate.

### Is a fitting worthwhile for one new wedge?

It can be useful because a fitting evaluates the golfer and the complete equipment setup. It is especially helpful when adding a wedge means removing another club or when your current carry notes reveal a gap without pointing to a clear solution.
