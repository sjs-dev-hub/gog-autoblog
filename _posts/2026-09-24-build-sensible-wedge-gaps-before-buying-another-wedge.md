## Start with the shot, not the club label.

An awkward number can make a new wedge seem obvious. Before choosing one, define the actual problem in plain terms: where the ball starts, where you want it to land, and which current clubs you considered but did not trust. “Between my two shortest clubs from this landing area” is useful. “I need a lob wedge” is a conclusion reached too early.

**Guild recommendation:** Write down two or three recurring on-course situations before comparing any new wedges.

## Make a simple coverage map.

List your current scoring clubs in order, including the loft stamped on each club if it is available. Then record the typical landing area from your normal swing and from a comfortable shorter swing. The aim is not to produce a perfect number. It is to see overlap, usable options, and blank spaces in your own pattern.

**Guild recommendation:** Treat a gap as real only when it appears repeatedly in your notes, not after one unusually good or poor shot.

## Separate a loft decision from a shot decision.

A stamped loft can help you organize the set, but the purchase decision should answer a playing problem. If two existing clubs create distinct, trusted landing areas with a controllable option between them, another wedge may merely duplicate a choice. If there is a repeated uncovered area and it matters on the courses you play, a new wedge becomes easier to justify.

**Guild recommendation:** Choose the prospective wedge category only after you can describe the uncovered shot it must cover.

## Use a fitting to check the whole setup.

If the map is unclear, or a prospective club changes the rest of your scoring setup, bring the complete picture to a qualified fitting conversation. The PGA of America describes club fitting as an evaluation of both the golfer and the complete equipment setup, which is a useful reminder not to assess one wedge in isolation.

**Guild recommendation:** Bring your current scoring clubs and your shot-map notes to a fitting so the decision can be considered in the context of the full set.

## Keep the final choice within the rules.

When replacing or adding a wedge, verify that the club is appropriate for the form of play you intend to enter. The USGA equipment rules govern club design and conformance, so a rules-conscious golfer should check the relevant requirements rather than rely on assumptions.

**Guild recommendation:** Confirm conformance through the applicable equipment rules before putting a new club into competition.

## The Three-Club Wedge Map.

Bring your three shortest scoring clubs, a notebook, and a range or practice area with several visible landing references. Choose a level, open area and use targets that allow you to watch where each ball lands safely.

<ol>

<li>Write down the club name or stamped loft for each club before hitting.</li>
<li>Hit five comfortable, ordinary shots with the first club toward the same general target area, recording where the balls land rather than judging only the best strike.</li>
<li>Repeat with the second and third clubs, keeping the same intent and noting the typical landing area for each one.</li>
<li>For each club, hit three deliberately shorter, controlled shots and record whether those shots create a usable additional landing area.</li>
<li>Review the notes and circle only the spaces between typical landing areas that no current club or controlled shot covers with confidence.</li>

</ol>

<p class="success-signal"><strong>What progress looks like:</strong> The plan succeeds when you can point to a repeated uncovered landing area, or when you can see that an existing club already supplies the shot you thought was missing.</p>

## The practical takeaway

- A sensible wedge setup begins with repeatable landing areas, not a guess based on a club category.
- Record both ordinary and controlled shots before deciding that a distance gap requires another wedge.
- A new wedge earns its place when it solves a recurring, observable shot problem that your current clubs do not cover comfortably.
- A complete-set fitting can help evaluate a wedge decision in context.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I know whether I have a real wedge gap?

You have a useful reason to investigate when repeated notes show an uncovered landing area between the shots you trust with your current scoring clubs.

### Should I choose a gap, sand, or lob wedge first?

Choose the category only after identifying the shot it must cover, because the category name alone does not describe your current coverage map.

### Do I need a fitting for one wedge?

A fitting can be worthwhile when the decision affects the rest of the setup, because the PGA of America frames fitting around the golfer and the complete equipment setup.

### Can I use an existing wedge for the in-between shot?

Yes, if your practice notes show that a controlled shot with a current club reaches the needed landing area with confidence.
